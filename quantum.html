01 / THE BASIC UNIT

A qubit is a state, not a tiny switch.

A classical bit is recorded as 0 or 1. An ideal qubit can be prepared in a state written |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. The two amplitudes α and β are complex numbers, and |α|² + |β|² = 1. Measuring in the 0/1 basis gives 0 with probability |α|² and 1 with probability |β|². This is the Born rule. Source ↗

A single measurement produces one ordinary bit. You cannot read out both amplitudes from one copy of an unknown qubit. To estimate probabilities, prepare the same state and measure it many times. Even states with identical 0/1 probabilities can behave differently later because their relative phase differs. Source ↗

02 / THE KEY TRICK

Quantum gates shape amplitudes.

A circuit initializes qubits, applies gates, then measures. Gates are reversible operations on the state vector until measurement. The H (Hadamard) gate makes |0⟩ → (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2. The X gate swaps 0 and 1 amplitudes. The Z gate changes the sign of the 1 amplitude. The S and T gates change its phase by π/2 and π/4. Source ↗

Phase alone does not change 0/1 measurement odds. A later gate can make amplitudes interfere: they add or cancel before probabilities are calculated. Compare these two circuits from |0⟩:

Why? After the first H, both outcomes have 50% probability. Z changes only the relative sign. The final H turns that hidden phase difference into a visible, deterministic answer. This is a small example of interference, the central design tool in quantum algorithms. Source ↗
Go deeper: the matrices and the phase

The state is a column vector (α, β)ᵀ. The gates in the lab are unitary matrices, so they preserve |α|²+|β|²=1: H = 1/√2 [[1,1],[1,−1]], X = [[0,1],[1,0]], and Z = [[1,0],[0,−1]]. S multiplies β by i; T multiplies β by eiπ/4. Gates act on the entire vector, including its complex phase.

Multiplying both amplitudes by the same unit-length complex number changes only the global phase and has no observable effect. Changing one relative to the other can change a later measurement. The presets show exactly that: HH|0⟩=|0⟩, while HZH|0⟩=|1⟩. Source ↗

03 / TWO QUBITS

Entanglement belongs to the pair.

Two qubits have four basis outcomes: |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩. In this exhibit the left digit is q₀. Apply H to q₀ in |00⟩, then a controlled-NOT (CNOT) that flips q₁ when q₀ is 1. The result is the Bell state (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2. It cannot be written as one independent state for q₀ times another for q₁. In general, n qubits need 2n basis amplitudes to describe their joint pure state, but one measurement does not reveal that whole vector. Source ↗

04 / PUTTING IT TO WORK

An algorithm must make useful answers likely.

With two qubits there are four possible answers. Grover's search gives a compact demonstration of amplitude amplification. Prepare equal amplitudes, mark one answer by reversing its phase, then apply a “diffusion” operation that reflects amplitudes about their mean. For this special four-item, one-solution example, one iteration makes the marked answer certain in the ideal model. For N unstructured items, the larger algorithm needs on the order of √N oracle queries rather than N, under the usual query-model assumptions. Source ↗

05 / THE ACTUAL MACHINE

Real qubits are physical and fragile.

A quantum processing unit uses a controllable physical system with two distinguishable basis states. Control pulses or light implement gates; readout hardware converts the final state into classical data. A conventional computer schedules the circuit, calibrates controls, and processes results. Hardware approaches differ substantially. Source ↗

Superconducting circuits

Microfabricated electrical circuits cooled near absolute zero. Microwave signals control their quantized energy states. Gates can be fast, but the states are sensitive to noise.

◎

Trapped ions

Charged atoms held in electromagnetic traps. Laser or electromagnetic control manipulates long-lived states, often with slower operations.

✳

Neutral atoms & photons

Neutral atoms can be held with laser light; photonic approaches encode information in light. Each platform has different tradeoffs for control, connectivity, loss, and scaling.

Noise is not just a wrong bit at the end. Imperfect preparation, gates, storage, and measurement can all alter the computation. Decoherence describes loss of quantum coherence through interaction with the environment. More gates and waiting generally create more opportunities for error, so circuit depth matters. Source ↗

06 / KEEPING INFORMATION ALIVE

Error correction checks a pattern without reading the secret.

One simple code spreads a logical qubit across three data qubits: α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ → α|000⟩ + β|111⟩. Parity checks on neighbors reveal where a single X (bit-flip) error occurred without directly measuring α or β. Real circuits use extra ancilla qubits to collect the syndrome. This code does not protect against Z (phase-flip) errors, and practical fault tolerance needs more sophisticated codes, repeated checks, and sufficiently reliable operations. Source ↗

07 / THE REALITY CHECK

What quantum computing can—and cannot—promise.

Where it may help

  • Quantum simulation: modeling some quantum systems such as molecules and materials is a natural fit.
  • Factoring: Shor's algorithm finds factors efficiently in theory using a quantum order-finding step; a sufficiently capable fault-tolerant machine would threaten RSA.
  • Unstructured search: Grover gives a quadratic query speedup, not an instant answer.

What it does not mean

  • Superposition does not let you read every possible answer. Measurement yields limited classical information.
  • Quantum computers are not faster for every task, and known algorithms do not make all hard problems easy.
  • The ideal simulations above omit calibration errors, decoherence, connectivity limits, and runtime costs.

The useful mental model is prepare → transform amplitudes and phase → make wanted outcomes more likely → measure → repeat. The hard engineering task is keeping that delicate process reliable long enough to matter. NIST ↗ IBM Quantum ↗
